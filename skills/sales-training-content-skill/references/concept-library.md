# Concept Library

The established sales and CS methodologies, cognitive science, and negotiation theory that inform training design. These are widely used, tried and tested concepts across the sales and customer success discipline.

---

## Qualification Frameworks

- **Foundational Fit Check**, confirming what the buyer can spend, who can approve it, what problem it solves, and when they need it resolved. Simple checklist qualification for transactional or SMB deals.
- **Enterprise Deal Map**, covering the measurable outcome targeted, who controls budget, decision criteria, decision process, the pain driving the initiative, and an internal champion. Enterprise qualification for complex multi stakeholder deals.
- **Extended Enterprise Map**, the above plus the formal procurement and paperwork route, and a clear read on competing options. Adds procurement/legal and competitive awareness.
- **Recurring Revenue Fit Check**, built around situation, pain, business impact, a critical event creating urgency, and how and when the decision will be made. Common in high velocity SaaS.

Select the qualification depth by deal complexity: the foundational check for simple/transactional, the enterprise map (extended where procurement is heavy) for enterprise, and the recurring revenue check for high velocity SaaS.

## Discovery Methodology

- Structured four stage questioning: background/context, problem surfacing, consequence/impact, value confirmation.
- Gap diagnosis: establish current state, define desired future state, quantify the gap, position the bridge.

## Core Sales Methodology Concepts

- Pain funnel questioning: uncover technical pain, then business pain, then personal/emotional stake, before proposing a solution.
- Insight led selling: teach the buyer something new about their business before pitching, tailor the message to their specific role, and take control of the sales process rather than reacting to a buyer led timeline.
- Value engineering: quantify cost of inaction and future state ROI in commercial terms the economic buyer understands.
- Consultative structure: diagnose before prescribing, resist leading with product.

## Negotiation and Influence Concepts

- Tactical empathy: naming and validating the counterpart's emotional state to lower defences before problem solving.
- Mirroring: repeating the last few words of a statement in a questioning tone to prompt elaboration.
- Labelling: verbalising the other party's presumed feelings or concerns to defuse them.
- Calibrated (open) questions: "how" and "what" questions that invite the counterpart to solve your problem for you.
- Accusation audit: pre-empting likely objections or negative perceptions early in a conversation to disarm them.
- Controlled vocal pacing: slow, calm, downward inflected delivery to project authority and reduce tension.

## Cognitive and Behavioural Science Foundations

- Dual process decision making: fast, automatic, emotionally driven judgement (defensive, says no first) versus slow, deliberate, analytical reasoning (where real decisions get made). Every objection handling and influence technique exists to shift the counterpart from fast to slow processing.
- Loss aversion: potential losses are weighted roughly twice as heavily as equivalent gains. Frame around cost of inaction, not just upside.
- Social proof: people look to the behaviour of similar others under uncertainty.
- Commitment and consistency: small early commitments increase the likelihood of larger later ones.
- Status quo bias: people default to inertia and need a credible trigger to consider change.
- Peak end rule: experiences are judged by their most intense point and their ending, not the average. Invest disproportionate care in openings and closings.

## Stakeholder and Deal Complexity Concepts

- Buying committees in complex B2B deals average six to ten people, each with different concerns. A deal is rarely lost to the whole committee at once, it stalls when one role (commonly the technical evaluator or a silent economic buyer) is left unaddressed.
- Separating the organisational result from the personal win for each stakeholder gives a sharper, more persuasive message than a single generic value proposition.
- Deal-level planning (the whole opportunity) and meeting-level planning (the next single conversation) are different disciplines and both are needed; planning only at the meeting level leaves the seller reactive to the buyer's agenda rather than driving their own.
- Buyers vary in their attitude to change (growth-seeking, problem-aware, complacent, or overconfident) and the same message does not land the same way with all four.

## Decision Psychology and Indecision

- A large share of lost enterprise deals are lost to no decision at all, not to a named competitor. Indecision behaves differently from a stated objection and needs a different diagnostic approach.
- Once a buyer has genuine purchase intent, their psychology shifts from "will this succeed" to "will this fail." Persuasion aimed at proving success adds little at this stage; reducing the perceived risk of failure moves the decision.
- Offering fewer, clearer recommended paths reduces buyer anxiety more effectively than presenting a wide menu of options, which counterintuitively increases stalling.

## Customer Success and Recurring Revenue Concepts

- Recurring revenue businesses recover the cost of acquiring a customer only after multiple renewal cycles, which means the post-sale journey (onboarding, adoption, renewal, expansion) carries more of the total business value than the initial sale itself.
- A customer health score is only useful if it is built from signals that are statistically correlated with actual past renewal and churn outcomes, rather than signals that are simply easy to collect.
- Leading indicators (usage trend, engagement frequency, executive sentiment) predict outcomes; lagging indicators (last quarter's data, a single past survey) only confirm what has already happened. A good model weights the former far more heavily.
- Qualitative relationship signals, such as a champion who stops asking forward-looking questions, often move before quantitative usage data does, and are a legitimate part of a health model even though they resist easy scoring.
- Segment health scoring by customer type. A slow, methodical adoption curve is a healthy sign for one segment and a warning sign for another.
- A business or account review that reports usage statistics reads as vendor administration. The same review, reframed around the customer's own stated business outcomes, reads as strategic partnership and is what actually protects renewal.

## Play and Engagement in Adult Learning

- Adults retain and apply skills better when practice happens under light competitive or playful pressure than when it happens through passive explanation. A rep who has laughed their way through a bad-QBR skit remembers the failure pattern far longer than one who was told about it.
- Mild stakes (a bell, a scoreboard, a daft forfeit, a time limit) lower the fear of looking foolish in front of peers, which is usually the real barrier to practising an unfamiliar technique out loud.
- Physical movement and exaggeration make abstract frameworks (a weighted health score, a stakeholder map, a stalling pattern) concrete and much easier to recall in the moment it's actually needed.
- Humour and competition are not a break from the training, they are the delivery mechanism. Content that only works when delivered solemnly usually is not sticking.

## Closing Concepts

- Two question close: ask if it makes sense to proceed, if not, ask what the other party would recommend as a next step. Removes pressure while maintaining momentum.
- Commitment culture: every meaningful interaction should end in a concrete next step. Absence of commitment is diagnostic information, not failure.

## Customer Success Concepts

- Value realisation tracking: map stated outcomes at point of sale to measurable, time bound proof points post onboarding.
- Risk and health scoring: composite indicators (usage, engagement, sentiment, sponsor stability) to flag churn risk early.
- Expansion timing: identify usage or outcome triggers that signal readiness for an upsell or cross-sell conversation, rather than running expansion on a fixed cadence.

## Service Recovery and Complaint Psychology

- Service recovery paradox: a complaint that is handled well can leave a customer *more* loyal than if nothing had gone wrong, because it proves the vendor keeps its promises under pressure. This only holds when the recovery is genuine and proportionate — over-persuading, over-compensating, or pushing for a "we're all good now" close reads as management of the customer rather than repair, and erases the gain.
- Emotion before information: a distressed customer is running on fast, defensive processing (see "Dual process decision making" above) and literally cannot absorb a solution until the feeling behind the complaint is acknowledged. Leading with the fix — however correct — is heard as dismissal. Acknowledge the emotion first, then the facts follow.
- The effort paradox: reducing the effort a customer has to expend to get their problem resolved predicts loyalty far more reliably than trying to "delight" them. Repeated explaining, channel switching, and chasing are what corrodes the relationship; a low-effort resolution is worth more than a lavish gesture bolted onto a painful process.
- Acknowledgement versus agreement: you can fully validate how a customer feels ("I can hear how frustrating this has been") without conceding fault or liability. Agents often withhold empathy because they think it admits blame — separating the two lets them de-escalate freely while staying accurate about what actually happened.
- The apology that lands: an effective apology is specific, owned, and paired with an action ("I'm sorry we billed you twice — I've reversed it now and here's when you'll see it"). The hollow "sorry for any inconvenience" signals a script and often inflames the customer because it acknowledges nothing concrete.
- De-escalation basics: use controlled vocal pacing (see the negotiation section) — slow, calm, downward-inflected delivery — name the emotion out loud (labelling) so the customer feels heard, and never match a rising tone. Tone-matching escalates; a steady counter-tone gives the customer's own arousal somewhere to settle.
- Blame psychology: defending the company, blaming another team, or blaming a system ("that's the other department, nothing I can do") destroys trust instantly, because the customer experiences the vendor as a single entity and hears an excuse. Own the *outcome* on the company's behalf without accepting *personal* fault — the customer needs someone to take responsibility for putting it right, not a confession.
- Live data grounding: use `qa_critical_errors` to surface real de-escalation and empathy failures, and `qa_scores` / `get_qa_report` to show where recovery attempts scored well or backfired on actual complaint calls.

## First-Contact Resolution and Diagnostic Rigour

- Capture in the customer's own words: an accurate problem statement, recorded in the customer's language before any reframing, beats a fast guess at what they "probably" mean. Premature relabelling sends the whole interaction chasing the wrong fault and forces the customer to repeat themselves — the opposite of first-contact resolution.
- Structured diagnosis: funnel deliberately from symptom to root cause (what happened, when, what changed, what has been tried) rather than pattern-matching to the first plausible answer. The cost of a premature solution is not just the wasted step — it teaches the customer the agent isn't really listening, which raises effort and erodes trust.
- Warm handover psychology: a clean escalation carries the full context, the diagnosis so far, and the customer's name and history to the next person, so the customer never re-explains. A cold transfer — dumping the customer into a queue to start again — is one of the highest-effort experiences there is and a leading driver of complaint escalation. Protect the customer from the seams in your own org.
- Confirming resolution: apply the peak-end rule (see above) to support. The *ending* disproportionately shapes how the whole interaction is remembered, so explicitly confirm the problem is solved, check nothing else is outstanding, and close on a clear, calm note. A correct fix delivered with an abrupt ending is remembered worse than the same fix with a deliberate, reassuring close.
- Live data grounding: use `qa_scores` and `list_qa_topics` to see where first-contact resolution and diagnostic steps are being scored, and `get_call` to pull the transcript moments where a premature solution or cold transfer occurred.

## Retention, Trust and Relationship Signals

- Trust equation intuition: perceived trustworthiness rises with credibility (do they know their stuff), reliability (do they do what they said), and intimacy (is it safe to be candid with them), and falls sharply with self-orientation (are they in it for themselves). For CSMs and account managers, the fastest way to raise trust is usually to *lower visible self-orientation* — leading with the customer's outcome rather than the renewal number.
- Reciprocity and proactive value: giving useful insight, a relevant benchmark, or a quiet win *before* asking for a renewal or expansion triggers reciprocity and reframes the ask as a continuation of value rather than an interruption. The account that only hears from its CSM at renewal time experiences the relationship as extractive.
- Reactive check-in versus proactive touch: a "just checking in" contact puts the work of finding a reason to talk onto the customer and signals the vendor has nothing to say. A proactive, outcome-anchored touch — tied to a specific result, milestone, or risk the customer cares about — is what actually builds standing and protects renewal. Same cadence, entirely different signal.
- Advocacy psychology: customers who help shape a plan, a rollout, or a success metric feel ownership of it and will defend the vendor internally when budgets are questioned, because the outcome is now partly *theirs*. Co-creation converts a satisfied user into an internal champion far more durably than a strong feature set alone. (Complements the "Collaborate" engagement concept rather than replacing it.)
- Live data grounding: use `get_competency_scores` and `competency_heatmap` to see where relationship and value-anchoring behaviours are landing, and `list_calls` filtered to CS/AM interactions to find real reactive-versus-proactive contrast examples.

## Manager and Coach Psychology

- Evidence over opinion: transcript-backed, criteria-first feedback removes defensiveness because the conversation is about what was observably said and done against a shared standard, not about the manager's impression of the rep. Grounding coaching in the platform's evidence-backed scoring and the 1–4 compounding competency scale turns "I think you were weak on discovery" into "here's the moment, here's the anchor you met and the one you didn't."
- The even-numbered scale forces a commitment: a 1–4 scale (unlike a 1–5) has no neutral middle to hide in, so every score is a decision that the behaviour was closer to the lower or the higher anchor. This same forced choice applies to the coach — it prevents the safe, uninformative "you're doing fine, about a 3" and demands a defensible call.
- Radical candour intuition: effective coaching sits where caring personally about the person meets challenging them directly on the work. Challenge without care is read as an attack; care without challenge is ruinous empathy that leaves the rep unimproved and quietly stalling. Both dials have to be up at once.
- Feedback timing and shape: coach close to the event while the call is still live in memory, and structure each note as situation–behaviour–impact (SBI) — the specific moment, the observable behaviour, and the concrete effect it had — so the feedback is specific, non-personal, and actionable rather than a vague verdict on the rep as a person.
- Live data grounding: use the 1–4 anchors from `get_framework` to keep coaching criteria-first, `get_assessment` and `get_competency_scores` to pull the evidence behind a score, and `coaching_plans` to anchor the next conversation in agreed, observable next steps.

## How This Feeds Training Design

1. Select the concepts relevant to the training topic from this library.
2. Cross reference against `framework-library.md` for the specific model or framework to teach for this topic.
3. Ground every framework in real client context (see "Contextual Intelligence Sourcing" in the main skill file) rather than generic textbook examples.
4. Name concepts descriptively based on what they do, rather than by any external source.
